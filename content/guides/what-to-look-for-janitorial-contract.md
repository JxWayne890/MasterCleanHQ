A janitorial contract should make the working relationship clear before the first visit. You should know what will be cleaned, how often, who handles problems, and what the quoted price includes. If those details live only in a sales conversation, the building manager and cleaning crew may begin with different expectations.

This guide is a practical purchasing checklist for offices, commercial buildings, churches, and other facilities in San Angelo and West Texas. It is not a substitute for legal review of a binding agreement. Master Commercial Clean operates under the legal name Master Clean HQ LLC, and a proposal should make the contracting business identity clear.

## Confirm the parties and the building

Check that the agreement names the correct customer, service provider, and property. A property manager may be signing for an owner or tenant, so confirm who has authority to approve the work and pay the invoices. Include service addresses and any separate billing address.

Name the operational contacts as well as the signer. The person handling daily access may differ from the person approving extra work. Establish where service questions, complaints, invoices, and urgent issues should go. A contract is easier to use when people know which contact can actually make a decision.

## Require a room and task schedule

“Janitorial services” is too broad to describe an entire scope. Attach a room list or service schedule that connects tasks to locations and frequency. Distinguish restrooms, offices, public areas, break rooms, and spaces that need special procedures.

Check for these details:

* Floors and surfaces included in routine service.
* Waste removal, recycling, and liner replacement responsibilities.
* Restroom fixtures, restocking, and consumable supply arrangements.
* Accessible desks, counters, and shared surfaces.
* Interior glass, dusting, and periodic detail tasks.
* Areas excluded because of access, equipment, or specialized requirements.

If the quote uses an existing checklist, make sure the correct version is attached. Write down how future scope changes are approved. That prevents an informal request from quietly changing the expected service without a corresponding conversation about time or price.

## Separate routine cleaning from periodic projects

Carpet extraction, floor finish work, detailed high dusting, post construction cleanup, and event resets can require different preparation and equipment. Ask which are included in the recurring agreement and which require a separate proposal.

For included periodic work, specify the frequency or condition that triggers it. For additional work, name the approval process before it begins. A useful extra work request identifies the location, task, price or pricing method, authorized person, and work window.

If the building needs an initial reset clean, describe it separately. Note permanent stains, worn finishes, damage, or maintenance issues at the starting walkthrough. Cleaning cannot be fairly assessed against a result that would require a repair or replacement.

## Check timing, access, and security

Define service days, work windows, holidays, closures, and event conflicts. An agreement for evening cleaning needs a plan for employees working late or meetings extending past the normal close. Include what happens when a room is locked or the building cannot be accessed.

Discuss keys, alarm codes, badge access, doors, parking, and storage. Agree on how access credentials are issued and returned. Put sensitive codes in a secure process rather than a widely shared checklist.

Ask how the provider manages personnel changes and who can enter restricted areas. If your organization requires background screening, identification, or training, define the exact requirement and how it will be verified. Do not assume those steps are included because another customer asked for them.

## Understand supplies and chemical responsibilities

The agreement should distinguish cleaning products and equipment from consumables such as toilet paper, soap, and liners. Identify who orders each item, where it is stored, and who notices when stock is low. Clarify whether purchases require approval and how they appear on invoices.

Ask how the provider selects products for your surfaces, maintains labels, and gives workers access to safety information. OSHA's [cleaning chemical guidance](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA3512.pdf) discusses worker protection, labels, training, and Safety Data Sheets. Use those topics to frame questions about the provider's procedures rather than assuming that a product name alone proves safe practice.

For special areas, confirm which requirements belong to your facility and which tasks the provider is equipped to perform. Ordinary office cleaning should not silently expand into clinical treatment areas or specialized remediation.

## Ask how quality will be checked

A service promise is more useful when it includes an inspection process. Ask who supervises the work, how issues are reported, and what correction process applies. Choose a shared method that is simple enough for the building contact to use.

An issue report should name the location, observable condition, date, and requested follow through. Agree on how urgent concerns differ from routine corrections. Avoid vague standards such as “perfectly clean” without examples of the conditions you expect.

Schedule an early review after service begins. Compare the actual building condition with the agreed checklist, then fix access or scope problems promptly. For ongoing service, decide how often the customer and provider will review recurring issues and changing needs.

## Read the price and payment terms together

Confirm the recurring charge, billing period, payment deadline, taxes if applicable, and any initial charge. Ask how a partial month, holiday closure, missed access, or requested extra visit will be handled. Compare the total scope rather than just the smallest monthly figure.

Look for provisions addressing future price changes, consumable purchases, fuel or supply charges, and extra work. Ask for unclear terms to be explained in writing. A reasonable question before signing is easier to resolve than an unexpected invoice after the work is complete.

## Verify insurance and review risk terms

Request current evidence of the insurance your organization requires and confirm that the named business matches the agreement. If your landlord or company has vendor requirements, share them before accepting a proposal. Ask the appropriate professional to verify coverage questions that affect your property or operations.

Read provisions about damage reporting, responsibility, indemnity, and limitations of liability carefully. Their meaning depends on the actual wording and your circumstances. If a term allocates significant risk, get qualified legal advice before agreeing rather than relying on a sales explanation.

## Know how renewal and cancellation work

Record the start date, initial term, renewal process, required notice, and where notice must be delivered. Check whether renewal is automatic and put any relevant review dates on your calendar. Ask what happens if a service problem remains unresolved.

Plan the end of service too: returning keys, disabling access, removing supplies, completing final work, and paying the final invoice. A clear transition process protects building operations even when the relationship ends amicably.
