A finished construction or remodel project is not ready for people just because the last trade has packed up. Fine dust settles on ledges, inside light fixtures, and along floor edges. Stickers and labels cling to new glass and fixtures. Paint specks, adhesive residue, and debris collect in corners nobody has looked at in weeks. If the cleanup scope is vague, those details tend to surface during a final walkthrough or on the first day staff move in.

This guide explains how to define a post construction cleaning scope before a commercial building handover, what to share with a cleaning provider, and how to plan the timing so the work fits your deadline. ## Decide where construction cleanup ends and handover cleaning begins

Construction projects often include more than one kind of cleanup. General contractors and trades usually remove their own debris, scrap, and packaging as work progresses. The cleaning that prepares a building for occupancy is a separate, more detailed job.

Before you request a quote, find out what your construction contract already covers. Ask the general contractor or project manager whether they plan any final cleaning, who removes dumpsters and bulk debris, and whether any trade is responsible for cleaning its own installed products. Writing this down prevents two common problems: paying twice for the same work, or discovering that nobody was assigned a task at all.

A cleaning provider should know whether they are arriving to a building with bulk debris already removed, or whether some hauling is expected. Those are very different jobs, and the quote should reflect which one you need.

## Understand the common phases

Many commercial projects plan cleanup in stages rather than as one visit at the end. The names vary, but the work usually falls into three steps.

The first step is a rough clean after major construction work. It focuses on removing loose debris, sweeping or vacuuming floors, and clearing space so finish trades can work.

The second step is the detailed clean once finishes are installed. This is where most of the work happens: dust removal from every reachable surface, cleaning fixtures and cabinets inside and out, removing labels and residue, and cleaning glass and floors.

The third step is a final touch up just before occupancy or inspection. Dust that settled after the detailed clean, footprints from late punch list work, and small marks get handled so the building is presentable on handover day.

Not every project needs all three. A small tenant improvement might combine them into one or two visits. Tell your provider which phases you want priced and when each one should happen.

## Build an area by area checklist

A written checklist keeps quotes comparable and gives everyone the same standard for the final inspection. Group tasks by area so nothing is left to assumption.

For floors, note the types and their condition:

- Vacuum carpet and check for adhesive, paint, or debris left by installers.
- Clean hard floors, including edges, corners, and thresholds where dust packs in.
- Identify whether new resilient flooring or sealed concrete needs any finish work, and confirm the flooring installer's care instructions before anything is applied.

For walls, doors, and trim:

- Dust baseboards, door frames, window sills, and ledges.
- Spot clean marks on walls and doors without damaging new paint.
- Clean door hardware and kick plates.

For glass and windows:

- Remove stickers and labels from interior glass, mirrors, and partitions.
- Clean interior window glass and frames.
- Decide separately whether exterior glass is included, since height and access change the work.

For restrooms and break rooms:

- Clean fixtures, partitions, counters, mirrors, and dispensers.
- Clean cabinets and drawers inside and out.
- Wipe appliance interiors and exteriors once they are installed and connected.

For overhead and building details:

- Dust reachable vents, return grilles, and light fixtures.
- Dust tops of cabinets, shelving, and other high surfaces within an agreed reach.
- Note any work that requires lifts or specialized access so it can be scoped separately.

## Plan for fine dust and safe work

Construction dust is the main reason post construction cleaning takes longer than an ordinary clean. It drifts into places that look clean at a glance and resettles after the first pass, which is why detailed cleaning often includes more than one round of dusting.

Some construction materials, such as concrete, masonry, and stone, can contain crystalline silica. OSHA's respirable crystalline silica standard for construction does not allow dry sweeping or dry brushing where it could contribute to silica exposure unless wet sweeping, HEPA filtered vacuuming, or other methods that minimize exposure are not feasible, and it limits the use of compressed air for cleaning surfaces. See [OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1153](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1153), paragraph (f). Ask the general contractor whether any active silica generating work is still happening, and schedule the detailed clean for after that work is complete.

Also ask the contractor about any areas that are restricted, freshly coated, or still curing. Cleaning products and methods should match each surface, and new finishes may come with manufacturer instructions about when and how they can first be cleaned.

## Schedule around your handover date

Post construction cleanup is usually tied to a deadline, such as a certificate of occupancy inspection, an owner walkthrough, a tenant move in, or an opening day. Work backward from that date when you plan.

Share the target handover date in your quote request, along with the current construction schedule. A provider needs to know when finish trades will be done, when power, water, and lighting will be available, and whether the building will be secured. Cleaning cannot be finished while painters, flooring crews, or electricians are still working in the same space, so leave room between the last trade and the final clean.

Build in time for a touch up visit after the punch list. Late repairs often leave dust and footprints in rooms that were already cleaned. Planning for that visit avoids a rushed request the day before handover.

## Share access and site rules

Construction sites often have rules that ordinary buildings do not. Tell your provider who the site contact is, how entry works, and whether there are check in procedures, required safety gear, or areas that cannot be entered. Mention where the crew can park, where they can stage equipment, and where trash from cleaning should go.

If utilities are not yet fully running, say so. Cleaning without water or adequate lighting is slower and may need to be scheduled differently.

## Explain the factors that shape the price

Post construction cleanup is usually priced as a project rather than a recurring rate. The main factors are the cleanable square footage, the amount of dust and debris remaining, the number of restrooms and kitchens, the amount of glass, the floor types, overhead work, the number of phases or visits, and how tight the deadline is.

Photos help a provider prepare, but a walkthrough is the most reliable way to see the actual condition. ## Agree on the final inspection

Decide before the work starts who will inspect the finished space and what standard they will use. Walk the building together with the checklist in hand, mark anything missed, and agree on when corrections will be made. A clear inspection step protects both the owner and the provider and gives you confidence on handover day.
