A walkthrough is the best chance a property manager has to make every cleaning bid describe the same building. When each provider sees the property on a different day, hears different priorities, and guesses at the parts nobody mentioned, the proposals that come back are hard to compare. A short checklist keeps the visit focused and gives every bidder the same information.

This guide is written for managers of offices, multi tenant buildings, schools, churches, and other commercial properties. Master Commercial Clean serves San Angelo and surrounding West Texas directly, and the same approach works whether you are hiring for the first time, rebidding a contract, or adding a new building to an existing service.

## Before the walkthrough: prepare one information sheet

Write down the basics before anyone arrives, then hand the same sheet to every provider. Shared facts reduce guesswork and make later comparison much easier.

Include:

* The property address and the building or suites included.
* Approximate square footage for each area in scope, and which floors are carpet, tile, sealed concrete, or another surface.
* Operating hours, tenant hours, and any times the building is closed.
* How many restrooms, break rooms, and entrances are in scope.
* The visit frequency you expect, or the range you are considering.
* Your desired start date and whether a current provider is still under contract.
* Who supplies paper products, soap, and trash liners.
* The person who will approve the scope and the person who will handle daily communication.

If you are not sure about frequency yet, say so. A good provider can explain how the scope changes with more or fewer visits, but only if you ask for that comparison in the same format from everyone.

## Entrances, lobbies, and common areas

Start where tenants and visitors start. Entrances collect the most soil, and in West Texas wind and dust can show up at doorways and on glass quickly. Walk the provider through each entrance and ask them to note what they see.

Point out:

* Entry mats and who owns them, whether they are leased, purchased, or provided by a separate service.
* Glass doors, sidelights, and interior glass that should be cleaned on a schedule.
* Lobby furniture, directories, mailbox areas, and reception counters.
* Elevators, stair rails, and corridor floors if the property has them.
* Any exterior areas such as sidewalks or patios, and whether those belong to cleaning, grounds care, or the landlord.

CDC guidance for community buildings recommends cleaning high touch surfaces regularly and cleaning other surfaces when they are visibly dirty. Its examples include door handles, stair rails, elevator buttons, and restroom fixtures, which are common area items a manager should name in the scope. See [CDC guidance on when and how to clean a facility](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/when-and-how-to-clean-and-disinfect-a-facility.html).

## Restrooms

Problems in restrooms are noticed quickly by tenants and visitors, so give them real time on the walkthrough. Count the fixtures, look at the dispensers, and talk about how busy each restroom gets on a normal day.

Ask each provider how they would handle:

* Toilets, urinals, sinks, counters, mirrors, partitions, and floors.
* Restocking, and whether stock is kept on site or delivered.
* Reporting leaks, broken dispensers, and plumbing problems.
* Restrooms used by the public or by tenants with very different schedules.

If one restroom is a constant problem, say so. That information helps a provider plan the visit order and the time needed.

## Tenant suites and occupied spaces

Multi tenant buildings need clear lines between what the landlord pays for and what each tenant handles. Settle that before the walkthrough if you can, and bring a list showing which suites are in scope.

Walk at least one typical suite and any suite with special conditions. Note locked offices, server or equipment rooms, confidential document areas, and spaces that need a specific product for the surface. Ask how the crew reports a room it could not enter, and how a tenant can raise an issue without waiting for the next monthly review.

## Break rooms, utility areas, and storage

Break rooms are easy to overlook during a quick tour. Show the provider each one and settle whether the scope covers counters and sinks only, or also appliance exteriors, microwave interiors, and refrigerator cleanouts. Decide who tells tenants before old food is thrown away.

Also show the provider:

* Where the crew can store equipment and supplies, and whether that room locks.
* The janitor closet or utility sink, and whether hot water is available.
* Dumpster locations and any recycling rules.
* Where wet floor signs and other safety items will be kept.

A provider without a place to store equipment or fill buckets may need to plan the work differently, which can change the proposal.

## Floors and periodic work

Hard floors and carpet often need two kinds of care: routine work at each visit and project work a few times a year. Walk the provider across each floor type and point out stains, worn finish, or damaged areas you already know about.

Ask each bidder to separate:

* Routine sweeping, mopping, and vacuuming included in each visit.
* Periodic work such as carpet extraction, machine scrubbing, or floor finish maintenance.
* Whether periodic work is priced inside the recurring rate or quoted separately.
* How much notice and clear space the periodic work needs.

Keeping these separate is one of the easiest ways to compare bids honestly. Two proposals can look far apart simply because one includes floor projects and the other does not.

## Access, keys, alarms, and security

Commercial cleaning often happens before opening, after closing, or in a quiet part of the day. Cover how the crew will get in and out safely.

Discuss:

* Keys, fobs, or codes, and how they are issued, tracked, and returned.
* Alarm arming and disarming, and who to call after a false alarm.
* Doors that must stay locked, and rooms the crew must never enter.
* Any background check or sign in process your property or tenants require.
* What happens when a crew member finds a door unlocked or something damaged.

## Questions to ask every bidder

Use the same questions with each provider so the answers line up side by side.

1. Which tasks are included at every visit, and which are weekly, monthly, or project work?
2. How do you schedule crews, and who is my main contact?
3. How do you check quality, and how can I report a problem?
4. What happens when a scheduled crew member is out?
5. Which supplies and equipment do you provide, and which do you expect us to provide?
6. What is excluded from this proposal?
7. How much notice do you need to change the scope or add a visit?

Ask for written answers, not just a price. A one page price with no scope gives you nothing to hold the provider to later.

## After the walkthrough: compare the proposals

When proposals arrive, line them up against your information sheet. Confirm each one covers the same areas, the same frequency, and the same periodic work. Mark anything a provider added or left out, and ask follow up questions before you decide.
