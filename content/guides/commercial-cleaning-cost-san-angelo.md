A useful commercial cleaning price starts with your building and the work it needs. Square footage matters, but it cannot tell the whole story. A quiet office with a few employees and a busy building with public restrooms may need very different amounts of attention, even when the floor plans are similar.

For businesses in San Angelo and the surrounding West Texas area, the most reliable way to budget is a written quote following a conversation and, when practical, a walkthrough. Master Commercial Clean provides commercial cleaning locally, including offices, schools, and commercial buildings. This guide explains what to prepare and how to judge whether a proposal covers the work you actually need.

## Why this guide does not publish a generic price

We have not published a standard MCC price range because a number without a defined scope can mislead you. The condition of the building, service schedule, access, supplies, and periodic floor work can all change the quote. An internet average may describe a different market or a different package of tasks.

Ask for a price that names the rooms, tasks, frequency, and exclusions. That gives you something you can approve and hold a provider accountable to. If you need an early budget before a walkthrough, describe your assumptions and request a preliminary estimate clearly identified as subject to inspection.

## The building details that affect your quote

Start with the areas that a cleaning crew will actually service. Gross building size may include storage rooms, mechanical spaces, or leased areas outside the requested scope. A floor plan with the included rooms marked is more useful than a single square footage figure.

Prepare these details:

* Approximate cleanable square footage and number of floors.
* Number of restrooms, fixtures, break rooms, and public entrances.
* Carpet, tile, concrete, resilient flooring, and other surface types.
* Employees, visitors, operating days, and unusually busy periods.
* Whether food is served, children use the space, or tenants share common areas.
* Areas requiring special access, restricted entry, or supervision.

In West Texas, mention how dust and tracked soil affect your entrances and floors. Show the provider where dirt collects instead of assuming that every room needs the same work. Also identify damaged flooring, old stains, or worn finishes. Cleaning and repairs are separate decisions, and a proposal should make that distinction clear.

## Frequency changes both the schedule and the workload

A building used every weekday may need restroom and waste service on each operating day while requiring some detail work less often. A small appointment office may have a different pattern. The right question is which tasks must happen between uses, which can rotate, and who handles issues between visits.

Ask the provider to separate routine service from periodic work. Routine service might include restroom cleaning, accessible surface wiping, waste removal, vacuuming, and damp mopping. Periodic work could include carpet extraction, machine floor care, high dusting, or detailed interior glass cleaning.

Do not assume fewer visits always produce the best value. If soil accumulates between visits, each visit may take longer or leave a result that does not meet your expectations. Start with your operating calendar and condition standards, then choose the schedule that supports them.

## Initial cleaning may need a separate scope

Taking over a building that has fallen behind is different from maintaining a building already in good condition. Before recurring service begins, walk through restroom buildup, floor edges, break room cabinets, carpet spots, and neglected corners together.

Ask whether the first service includes a reset clean or whether that work is priced separately. Define what the initial service will address and what it cannot restore. Take dated photos of existing damage or permanent staining so everyone has the same starting point.

If you are moving into a renovated space, ask about construction dust, adhesive residue, stickers, and debris specifically. A recurring janitorial quote should not be mistaken for a post construction cleanup proposal.

## Supplies, access, and special tasks can change the total

Clarify who buys restroom paper, soap, liners, and other consumables. Ask whether the provider supplies cleaning products and equipment, whether restocking is included, and how additional supply purchases are approved. A low service price can be difficult to compare if another proposal includes consumables.

Access also matters. A crew that can enter after closing has a different work window from one that must stop around meetings or visitors. Discuss keys, alarm codes, parking, water access, storage, elevator rules, and the person available if access fails.

List special requests individually. Examples include cleaning inside refrigerators, moving furniture, exterior windows, floor finish work, event cleanup, and urgent visits. Ask for approval rules before extra charges occur. The goal is a clear total cost, with optional work you can choose deliberately.

## Compare proposals using the same checklist

Send each bidder the same room list and task schedule. Then compare what is included before comparing the monthly totals. A proposal that says only “general cleaning” leaves too much room for different expectations.

For each proposal, check:

* The number of scheduled visits and which holidays are excluded.
* Specific tasks and the frequency for each room type.
* Periodic work included in the recurring price.
* Supplies and consumables included or billed separately.
* Inspection, issue reporting, and correction procedures.
* Initial cleaning, extra work, and cancellation terms.

For a quick comparison, divide the quoted recurring total by the included visits for that same period. Use this only to understand the proposal, since visits may contain different tasks. Also compare the annual scope so quarterly or annual floor work does not disappear from the decision.

## Reduce cost by fixing scope problems


The most useful savings often come from removing confusion. Identify rooms that are rarely used, agree on accessible desk surfaces, and set a simple process for reporting spills or supply shortages. Keep storage out of the cleaning path and coordinate access so scheduled work can start on time.

Avoid cutting essential restroom or waste tasks just to make a monthly figure look smaller. Instead, discuss rotating low priority detail tasks, adjusting visits in unused areas, and scheduling periodic projects around real building use. A provider can price those choices more accurately when they are written down.

## What to send when you request a quote

Send your building address, approximate size, business hours, requested start date, and preferred service days. Include a short description of the problem you want solved, such as inconsistent restrooms, dusty entrances, or a provider transition. Photos and a floor plan help, but they do not replace an honest discussion about condition and expectations.
