Changing janitorial companies is often the right decision, but the switch itself can create problems. Restrooms go a night without service, keys are still in the old crew's hands, or nobody knows which supplies are on order. A little planning keeps your building clean while one provider leaves and the next one starts.

This guide walks through the steps a business, property manager, or facilities team can take to replace a cleaning provider without a gap in service. ## Start with your current contract

Before you contact a new provider or tell your current one anything, read the agreement you already have. Look for the term, the renewal date, the notice requirement, and how notice must be delivered. Some contracts renew automatically unless notice arrives in writing within a set window. Others allow cancellation at any time with a stated notice period.

Also look for anything the current provider owns inside your building. Equipment, supply stock, dispensers, and chemical storage may belong to them, and they may remove those items when service ends. Note any terms about final invoices, deposits, or charges for early termination.

If the contract language is unclear, ask your own advisor before acting on it. Your goal is to know the last date the current provider is obligated to clean, so the new start date can follow it directly.

## Write down what is going wrong

A clear record of the problems you want solved helps in two ways. It tells a new provider what matters most to you, and it keeps the next contract from repeating the same gaps.

Keep a short log for a week or two before the change. Record the date, the area, and what was missed or done poorly. Common examples include restrooms that are not restocked, trash left overnight, dusty entrances, floors with buildup along the edges, and break rooms that are only partly cleaned. Dated photos make the record more useful.

Separate service problems from scope problems. If a task was never in the contract, the current provider may not have been failing. That task simply needs to be written into the new scope.

## Build a scope the new provider can price

Before you request proposals, describe what your building needs in plain terms. List the rooms and areas that should be serviced, the tasks for each one, and how often each task should happen. Mark areas that are off limits or need supervised access.

Include the details that change the workload:

* Operating days and hours, plus busy seasons or events.
* Number of restrooms, break rooms, and public entrances.
* Floor types, such as carpet, tile, and sealed concrete.
* Periodic work, such as carpet extraction or floor finish maintenance.
* Who supplies paper, soap, liners, and cleaning products.

Send the same scope to every company you consider, so proposals can be compared fairly. ## Schedule a walkthrough before you give notice

A walkthrough lets the new provider see the actual condition of your building, the access points, and the spots that need extra attention. It also gives you a chance to judge how carefully the provider listens and asks questions.

Try to schedule the walkthrough and receive a written proposal before you send notice to the current company. That way you are not left with a notice deadline approaching and no confirmed replacement. Ask the new provider directly whether they can start on the date you need, and get that start date in writing.

Discuss whether the building needs an initial deep clean before routine service begins. A building that has fallen behind may need extra work on restrooms, floors, and corners first. Agree on what that first service covers and whether it is priced separately from recurring cleaning.

## Plan the handoff date

Choose a handoff date so the last night of old service and the first night of new service fit together with no empty days. If your contract allows it, a short overlap is safer than a gap, especially for buildings that cannot go a day without restroom service.

Confirm these items for the handoff:

* The last scheduled visit from the current provider.
* The first scheduled visit from the new provider.
* Who on your staff is the contact person that week.
* What happens if the first visit has an access problem.

Avoid scheduling the switch right before a major event, an inspection, or a holiday week, when a missed night would be more visible.

## Collect keys, codes, and access

Access is where many transitions go wrong. Make a list of every key, badge, fob, and alarm code the current provider holds. Collect physical keys on or before the last day of service, and confirm the count against your records.

Change alarm codes and door codes that the outgoing crew used, and remove any building access the outgoing provider no longer needs. Then issue new access to the incoming provider and record who received what.

Walk the new crew through the alarm system, lighting, locked rooms, janitor closets, water access, trash and recycling locations, and parking. Write down any building rules, such as areas that must stay locked or doors that must not be propped open.

## Sort out supplies and equipment

Find out which supplies and equipment belong to your business and which belong to the outgoing provider. If their dispensers or stock will leave with them, arrange replacements before the first night of new service.

Decide who buys restroom paper, hand soap, liners, and other consumables going forward. If the new provider will supply them, confirm what is included in the price. If your business will keep buying them, set a simple way for the crew to report when stock runs low.

## Watch the first weeks closely

The first few weeks show whether the new provider understands your building. Walk the space after the first several visits and compare what you see with the written scope. Share concerns quickly and specifically, naming the area and the task.

Ask the new provider how they handle inspections, issue reports, and corrections. Agree on one contact person on each side and the best way to reach them. Keep using your problem log, so you can see whether the issues that started the change are actually resolved.
