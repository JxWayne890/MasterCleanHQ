A small office does not need a complicated cleaning system. It needs a clear list of tasks, a schedule based on how the space is used, and a reliable way to catch missed work. Without those basics, employees may assume the cleaner handles a task while the cleaner assumes it belongs to the office.

This checklist helps you define the work before requesting a quote or reviewing an existing service. It suits ordinary business offices, shared workspaces, and customer reception areas. Medical treatment rooms, food production, and other regulated spaces need their own procedures. ## Start with a room list and clear responsibilities

Walk through your office and list each area: entrance, reception, workstations, meeting rooms, restrooms, break room, storage, and internal corridors. For each area, decide what your staff will handle and what belongs in a cleaning agreement.

Staff might clear personal items, wash their own dishes, and dispose of food. The cleaning crew might handle waste bins, accessible counters, floors, and restrooms. Neither arrangement is universal. Write it down so the schedule reflects the work someone has actually agreed to do.

Identify restricted rooms, confidential documents, delicate equipment, and surfaces that need a specific product. Tell the crew which desks can be cleaned and how to treat items left out. A clear desk policy makes cleaning easier, but nobody should move confidential files simply to finish a checklist.

## Choose frequency by use and condition

An office open five days a week with steady visitors has different needs from a quiet space used by appointment. Restrooms, food waste, shared touchpoints, and entrances deserve attention based on actual use. Less used rooms can follow a different schedule.

CDC recommends regular cleaning of frequently touched surfaces in community facilities and cleaning other surfaces when visibly dirty. It also distinguishes routine cleaning from disinfection needed for particular circumstances. See [CDC's facility cleaning guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/when-and-how-to-clean-and-disinfect-a-facility.html). Use that distinction to request specific tasks rather than vague promises to “sanitize everything.”

The schedule below is a planning starting point. Increase or reduce tasks after observing your office, and follow product and surface instructions. A cleaning provider should help you turn the list into an agreed scope.

## Each service visit: entrances and reception

Reception sets the first impression and collects much of the soil entering the building. In San Angelo, inspect mats and doorways after windy days or dirty foot traffic, then adjust work to what you find.

* Remove visible litter and empty assigned waste bins.
* Clean entrance glass where fingerprints or smudges are visible.
* Wipe accessible reception counters and designated shared surfaces.
* Vacuum mats and carpeted traffic lanes.
* Clean hard floors with a suitable method and protect the area while wet.
* Note damaged mats, leaks, or trip concerns for the office manager.

Decide whether exterior sidewalks, exterior windows, and parking areas belong to your landlord, staff, or a separate service. They should not be assumed part of ordinary interior office cleaning.

## Each service visit: workstations and meeting rooms

Give cleaners access to the surfaces included in the agreement. Establish whether staff or the provider handles keyboards, phones, monitors, and other electronics, and follow equipment instructions. Do not spray a product into office equipment.

* Empty designated waste and recycling bins using the agreed separation rules.
* Wipe accessible desks, tables, and counters within scope.
* Clean assigned door handles, switches, and shared contact points.
* Vacuum or clean floors, including visible debris under accessible desks.
* Reset meeting room chairs if that task is included.
* Record locked rooms or blocked surfaces that prevented work.

The checklist should distinguish a reachable table from a crowded desk. Agree on how the crew reports inaccessible areas so the office can correct them before the next visit.

## Each service visit: restrooms

Restrooms need their own complete task list. A floor that looks clean does not tell you whether fixtures, dispensers, and supplies received attention. Name the surfaces and the restocking responsibility.

* Clean toilets, urinals, sinks, and assigned surrounding surfaces.
* Clean mirrors, counters, handles, and dispensers as appropriate.
* Address visible marks and soil on partitions and doors.
* Empty waste bins and replace liners when needed.
* Replenish paper and soap from the agreed supply stock.
* Clean floors and inspect edges and corners.
* Report leaks, broken dispensers, persistent odors, or blocked fixtures.

Keep restroom tools and procedures distinct from break room work. Ask your provider how equipment is stored and how the crew avoids carrying soil between areas. If an issue involves bodily fluids or a suspected hazard, use an established incident procedure with properly trained personnel.

## Each service visit: break rooms

Food residue and unclear ownership can make break rooms a recurring frustration. Specify whether the provider cleans only accessible counters and sinks or also appliance interiors and dishes.

* Remove food waste and clean assigned waste containers when needed.
* Clean accessible tables, counters, sinks, and appliance handles.
* Wipe approved exterior appliance surfaces.
* Clean visible spills and crumbs within scope.
* Vacuum or clean floors using the correct method.
* Check that supplies and personal belongings are not blocking work.

Schedule refrigerator cleanouts with advance notice to staff. Name the person who decides what can be discarded. Treat microwaves, coffee equipment, dishwashers, and food contact surfaces according to their instructions and the agreed service plan.

## Weekly and monthly detail work

Some tasks do not need to happen at every visit, but they still need a place on the schedule. Assign a rotating day or a separate project so detail work does not disappear behind routine tasks.

Weekly planning can include dusting reachable ledges, cleaning floor edges, checking carpet spots, and cleaning assigned interior glass. Monthly reviews can cover reachable vents, baseboards, less used rooms, waste container condition, and dust behind furniture that can be moved safely.

Carpet extraction, machine floor care, high access work, and exterior windows deserve a separate scope based on condition and material. Ask what the work includes, whether furniture movement is included, how long the space must remain clear, and whether the quoted recurring price covers it.

## Keep a simple quality check

Choose one office contact and one provider contact. After service, inspect a few repeatable points: restroom supplies, entrance mats, break room counters, floor edges, and assigned bins. Record the location and condition when something is missed.

Review patterns instead of treating each complaint as unrelated. Repeated empty soap dispensers may indicate a supply ordering problem. A room missed every week may require a new access arrangement. Update the written scope when office use changes, such as adding staff or opening the space to more visitors.

## Turn the checklist into a cleaning quote

Share your room list, approximate square footage, operating days, desired frequency, and priorities. Include your preferred start date and any access restrictions. Ask the provider to identify exclusions and periodic tasks so you can compare proposals fairly.
